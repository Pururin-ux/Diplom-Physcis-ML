\section{Introduction}

Shape control is an appealing route for studying and designing quantum-dot
spectra. In model systems, the confinement boundary can be changed cleanly,
low-energy levels can be computed directly, and surrogate models can be trained
on repeated spectral calculations. These ingredients make a square-lattice
tight-binding model of superelliptic quantum dots a useful controlled benchmark:
the Hamiltonian is simple, the geometry family is interpretable, and the
calculated spectra retain the finite-size and boundary effects that motivate
shape-dependent analysis. At the same time, this simplicity creates a demanding
test of any proposed shape objective. If an apparent signal is already explained
by size, area, aspect ratio, symmetry, degeneracy, boundary pixelation, or lead
coupling, then it should not be presented as inverse design or as a new physical
effect.

This article is a limitations paper. It summarizes existing outputs from a
diploma project and subsequent article-extension checks, all within the same
finite superellipse tight-binding family. The purpose is not to enlarge the
parameter space, repair failed directions, or promote a new objective. Instead,
the paper asks what remains after simple physics baselines and methodological
checks are applied to several attractive routes: closed-spectrum surrogate
modeling, one-shot spectral inverse screening, magnetic ranking crossover,
tight-binding to continuum residual analysis, tight-binding-only self-scaling,
and open transport.

The need for such an audit is practical. Low-energy levels of confined systems
are strongly governed by dimensional scaling; near the bottom of a square-lattice
tight-binding band, the dispersion has the expected quadratic form. Classical
surrogate-modeling practice also warns that flexible models must be compared
against simple, structured baselines rather than weak or randomly chosen
alternatives~\cite{bishop2006prml,hastie2009elements,forrester2008surrogate,queipo2005surrogate}.
For spatially or hierarchically structured data, validation splits must reflect
the intended extrapolation or interpolation task~\cite{roberts2017crossvalidation}.
In the present project, these points are not abstract methodological concerns:
they decide whether a shape-dependent claim survives at all.

The main message is therefore conservative. In the tested square-lattice
tight-binding model of finite superelliptic quantum dots, several plausible
shape, ML, continuum-reference, and transport objectives do not support positive
claims after baseline checks. This does not imply that geometry-based quantum
design, machine learning, finite-size analysis, or transport studies are useless
in general. It says only that, in this controlled family and with the tested
protocols, baseline-first tests are necessary and often decisive.
